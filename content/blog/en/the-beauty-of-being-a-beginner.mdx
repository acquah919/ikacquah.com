_Somewhere along the way, we became so obsessed with becoming successful that we forgot how beautiful it is to be becoming._

I recently came across a video that genuinely made me stop and think.

And if you know me, you know I enjoy reflecting on life, particularly those little things we often overlook while chasing our ambitions.

The video raised a simple but uncomfortable question: Why has it become so difficult to enjoy being a beginner?

Why do we feel embarrassed when we don't know something? Why do we expect ourselves to be good at things we've only just started? And when exactly did we collectively agree that everyone must have their life figured out by a certain age?

I have been thinking about this, and I suspect the internet has a lot to answer for.

Not because access to information is bad. Quite the opposite. But because we've somehow convinced ourselves that having access to information means we should already have mastered everything. And I think many of us, myself included, have fallen into that trap.

## We Have All the Information, but Somehow We Feel More Behind

Think about it.

- You want to learn programming? Thousands of YouTube tutorials.
- You want to lose weight? Someone has a 30-day transformation programme.
- You want to become financially independent? A 23-year-old on TikTok is explaining how you can become a millionaire before breakfast.
- You want to pursue a PhD? Someone on LinkedIn has already published 15 papers, won three international awards, secured a postdoctoral fellowship, and somehow still finds time to post motivational quotes every morning.

Meanwhile, you're sitting in front of your laptop wondering why your Python code has refused to run for the past four hours.

Wonderful. 😂

I can relate to that last one more than I'd like to admit.

During my PhD research in medical physics, particularly while working with deep learning models for generating synthetic CT images from MRI, I had my fair share of moments when things simply refused to work.

You read a paper, and the authors make their methodology sound so straightforward. You try implementing something similar, and suddenly your computer decides that cooperation is optional.

Sometimes the model trains beautifully, but the results look nothing like what you expected. Other times, you spend hours troubleshooting an error, only to discover that the problem was something embarrassingly simple.

And then you open LinkedIn.

Someone has just published another paper on artificial intelligence. Someone else has received a prestigious research grant. Another person has announced a new academic appointment.

And suddenly, instead of appreciating the progress you've made, you begin wondering whether you're doing enough.

**That's the strange thing about our generation. We have more access to knowledge than ever before, yet we sometimes feel less accomplished because of it.**

## Social Media Has Made Everyone Look Like They're Winning

One of the most dangerous things we do is compare our beginnings with other people's finished products.

You publish your first paper, and instead of celebrating, you discover someone your age has published 40.

You finally secure your first job, and someone you attended university with has just announced their second promotion.

You start exercising, and Instagram immediately introduces you to a gentleman whose muscles appear to have their own muscles.

At this point, even your own achievements begin to look suspiciously like underachievement.

But here's what we forget.

**We are comparing our private struggles with other people's public victories.**

- We see the graduation photographs, but not the sleepless nights.
- We see the published papers, but not the rejection emails.
- We see the successful businesses, but not the failed investments.
- We see the transformation, but not the days when someone almost gave up.

I completed my PhD, and while that achievement means a great deal to me, I can tell you that the journey was far more complicated than any graduation photograph could ever communicate.

There were moments of uncertainty, frustrating research challenges, corrections, and the constant feeling that there was still so much more to learn.

But looking back, those difficult moments were not interruptions to the journey. They were part of what made the achievement meaningful.

And perhaps that's what we need to remind ourselves more often.

**The finished product tells people what you achieved. The process tells the story of who you became.**

## What My PhD Journey Taught Me About Being a Beginner

When I began my doctoral research, I knew the direction I wanted to pursue. I was interested in applying deep learning to medical imaging, particularly generating synthetic CT images from MRI for radiotherapy treatment planning.

It was exciting.

The idea that artificial intelligence could contribute to improving radiotherapy workflows and potentially reducing the need for additional CT imaging was something I genuinely wanted to explore.

But having a research idea and successfully executing it are two very different things.

There is a significant difference between reading about a U-Net, Pix2Pix, or CycleGAN architecture and getting those models to perform well on real clinical imaging data.

And when you're dealing with medical images, preprocessing, image registration, model training, validation, and quantitative evaluation, there are plenty of opportunities for things to go wrong.

Sometimes the difficulty isn't even the model. It's the data. Or the registration. Or the computing resources. Or that one error message that appears to have been written specifically to test your patience.

But every challenge forced me to learn something. Every failed attempt made me reconsider an assumption. Every correction improved my understanding.

And somewhere along the way, I realised something important.

**The PhD was not simply about producing a thesis. It was about becoming a researcher capable of asking better questions, handling uncertainty, and finding solutions.**

The title of Doctor is wonderful, of course. I certainly won't pretend otherwise. 😂

But the growth that took place before that title was earned is something I value just as much.

And that is the beauty of being a beginner.

You don't always notice yourself changing while the process is happening. Sometimes you only recognise how far you've come when you look back.

## Even Lecturers Are Still Learning

One of the things I enjoy about teaching physics is watching students gradually move from confusion to understanding.

A student struggles with a concept in quantum mechanics, electronics, or nuclear physics.

You explain it one way. Nothing. You try another approach. Still nothing.

Then you introduce an example, draw a diagram, or relate the concept to something familiar. Suddenly, you see that expression. The moment when everything begins to make sense.

I find those moments incredibly satisfying.

But there's something else I wish more students understood.

**University is not a place where you're expected to arrive knowing everything. It is a place where you're supposed to learn.**

Yet some students are afraid to ask questions because they don't want their colleagues to think they're unintelligent.

Imagine paying school fees, attending lectures, sitting in a room specifically designed for learning, and then deciding not to ask a question because someone might discover that you don't know something.

My brother, that is literally why you're there! 😂

And the funny thing is that lecturers are not exempt from this process.

I still encounter concepts I need to revisit. I still read new research that challenges my understanding. I still learn new techniques, particularly in artificial intelligence and medical imaging.

Sometimes teaching a topic even forces me to understand it more deeply than I did before.

So when I encourage my students to ask questions, I'm not speaking as someone who has finished learning. I'm speaking as someone who understands that learning never really ends.

**Being knowledgeable doesn't mean you have stopped being a beginner. Sometimes it simply means you've become comfortable beginning again.**

## Even Manchester United Has Taught Me Something About the Process

Now, before anyone accuses me of turning every conversation into football, allow me to make a small confession.

Being a Manchester United supporter has taught me a thing or two about patience, hope, disappointment, and the importance of trusting a process.

Although, to be fair, I sometimes wonder whether the process itself knows where it's going. 😂

Every season comes with renewed optimism.

New signings. Fresh tactical ideas. Players returning from injury. The belief that this might finally be the season everything falls into place.

Then football begins.

And suddenly, we're back to discussing defensive errors, missed opportunities, and why a team with so much potential occasionally behaves as though the players met for the first time in the dressing room.

But we keep supporting. We keep hoping. We keep believing that improvement is possible.

And that brings me to my good friend, Investor Purity.

If you've read my [previous football blog](/en/blog/football-is-back), you already know that Purity has appointed himself the unofficial auditor of Manchester United's misfortunes.

This gentleman has a remarkable ability to detect when United are losing.

I genuinely suspect he has a special notification system that alerts him whenever we concede a goal.

Sometimes I wonder whether he watches football because he enjoys the sport or simply because Manchester United might lose.

And naturally, when that happens, my phone becomes his preferred destination.

But there's something amusing about it all.

Supporting a football club, much like pursuing a meaningful goal in life, requires you to accept that not every day will bring victory.

There will be frustrating afternoons. There will be moments when progress seems painfully slow. There will be people like Purity waiting patiently to remind you of every setback. 😂

But you don't abandon something you love simply because the journey becomes difficult.

You learn to appreciate the victories, endure the disappointments, and maintain hope for what comes next.

Of course, Manchester United should not interpret this philosophical reflection as permission to continue losing matches.

There are limits to every process, please. 😂

And Purity, if you're reading this, remember that even great teams go through difficult periods.

Our comeback is loading.

Admittedly, the internet connection appears to be rather slow.

## The Dangerous Obsession With Shortcuts

Another thing I've been thinking about is how obsessed we've become with speed.

We want to master skills quickly. We want businesses that become profitable immediately. We want successful careers without uncomfortable learning curves. We want research results without frustrating experiments.

And increasingly, we want technology to remove every difficult part of the process.

As someone who works with artificial intelligence, I find this particularly interesting.

I genuinely believe AI has enormous potential to improve education, research, healthcare, and many other areas of our lives. I've seen firsthand how useful these technologies can be.

But there is an important difference between using technology to support learning and using it to avoid learning altogether.

An AI system can help you write code. But can you identify when the code is wrong?

It can help you analyse data. But do you understand what the results mean?

It can help you write an academic paper. But can you defend the methodology and explain the scientific reasoning behind your conclusions?

These questions matter.

Because while technology can accelerate certain tasks, it cannot automatically give us the experience, judgement, and understanding that come from genuine engagement.

**Not every struggle is an obstacle that needs to be eliminated. Sometimes the struggle is the education.**

And I think that's something worth remembering in an age where almost everything is advertised as faster, easier, and more convenient.

## Stop Postponing Your Happiness Until You Arrive

Perhaps the most powerful part of the video was the reminder that we shouldn't reserve all our happiness for some future achievement.

And I must admit, this one hit home.

I have always believed in setting goals.

As an academic, there's almost always something you're working towards.

- A degree.
- A publication.
- A conference presentation.
- A research collaboration.
- A grant application.
- A promotion.
- Another opportunity.

And the interesting thing is that the moment you achieve one goal, another appears. You complete your PhD, and before you've properly finished celebrating, you're already thinking about publications, postdoctoral opportunities, and the next stage of your academic career. It can become an endless cycle.

There's nothing wrong with ambition. I certainly don't intend to stop pursuing new opportunities.

**But I am increasingly convinced that we must learn to enjoy our lives while working towards our ambitions.**

Not after.

While.

- Celebrate the paper that finally gets accepted.
- Celebrate the student who tells you that your lecture helped them understand something.
- Celebrate the research problem you finally solve.
- Celebrate the opportunity to travel, meet new people, and experience different environments.
- Celebrate the ordinary evenings when you can sit down, watch football, and forget about academic deadlines for 90 minutes.

Although, depending on Manchester United's performance, those 90 minutes may introduce an entirely different category of stress.

And if Purity calls immediately after the final whistle, the stress becomes a full research project. 😂

But you get the point.

Life is not simply a collection of major achievements. It is also made up of those small, seemingly ordinary moments that we sometimes fail to appreciate.

## You Are Not Late to Your Own Life

One thing I've come to appreciate is that people have different timelines.

- Someone completes university at 21.
- Someone else finishes at 30.
- Someone becomes a professor at 35.
- Someone discovers their passion at 50.
- Someone starts a successful business after retirement.

These stories are different, but difference doesn't automatically mean delay.

In academia, it's particularly easy to feel behind.

There will always be someone with more publications. Someone with more citations. Someone with a bigger research grant. Someone working at a more prestigious institution. Someone who achieved a milestone earlier than you did.

But if you spend your entire career measuring your progress against other people's achievements, you may never appreciate your own.

I have learned to remind myself that the goal is not simply to accumulate academic titles or publications. It is also to contribute meaningfully to science, support students, develop useful research, and hopefully leave something valuable behind.

And that takes time. It takes patience. It takes mistakes. It takes the willingness to remain a student even when you have become a lecturer.

**You are allowed to be a work in progress without feeling like a failed finished product.**

I think that's a message many of us need to hear.

## A Final Thought: Enjoy Becoming

If there's one thing I want you to take away from this reflection, it's this:

**Don't become so obsessed with the person you want to be that you forget to appreciate the person you're becoming.**

- Start the research.
- Learn the new skill.
- Apply for the opportunity.
- Write the first article.
- Build the business.
- Ask the question.

Be terrible at something new, if that's what it takes to eventually become good at it.

And please, don't let someone's carefully edited success story convince you that your own journey is inadequate.

You don't have to know everything. You don't have to get everything right the first time. You don't have to achieve everything before a particular age. You simply have to keep learning, keep growing, and remain open to the possibilities ahead.

Looking back at my own journey, from studying physics to working in medical physics, pursuing doctoral research, and teaching the next generation of students, I can appreciate how much of my growth happened during periods when I was still trying to figure things out.

And the truth is, I'm still figuring things out.

There are new research questions I want to explore. New skills I want to develop. New opportunities I hope to pursue. New experiences I want to have. And, hopefully, a Manchester United season that doesn't require extraordinary levels of emotional resilience.

Purity, I'm still waiting for that one. 😂

But whatever happens, I don't want to spend my entire life waiting for the next achievement before allowing myself to be happy.

Because the journey isn't merely preparing us for something meaningful.

**The journey itself is meaningful.**

So, wherever you are in life right now, give yourself permission to be a beginner. Give yourself permission to make mistakes. Give yourself permission to grow at your own pace. And above all, give yourself permission to enjoy your life while you're still becoming.

**Life doesn't begin when you finally become successful. Life is happening right now. Don't miss it while waiting to arrive.**
